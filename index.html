🔬 Experimental Procedure

Step-by-step guide for biodiesel production using ultrasonic transesterification

1

Preparation Phase

Time: 10-15 minutes

  • Filter castor oil to remove impurities
  • Measure 50 mL of castor oil into a clean, dry flask
  • Measure 8 mL of methanol and 2.5 g NaOH separately
  • Keep 5-6 drops of distilled water ready
2

Catalyst Preparation

Time: 5 minutes

  • Dissolve 2.5 g NaOH in 8 mL methanol (in a beaker)
  • Stir until completely dissolved to form sodium methoxide
  • Add 5-6 drops of distilled water as used in the run
3

Mixing & Ultrasonic Treatment

Time: 20-30 minutes

  • Pour sodium methoxide solution into the castor oil
  • Place the flask in the ultrasonicator
  • Sonicate the mixture for 20-30 minutes
  • After sonication, keep the mixture on a stand for 24 hours
4

Phase Separation

Time: 24 hours (settling)

  • Allow the mixture to settle undisturbed on the stand
  • Two distinct layers form: biodiesel (top) and glycerol (bottom)
  • Observed volumes: biodiesel 32 mL and glycerol 28 mL
5

Optional Washing

Time: 15-20 minutes (multiple cycles)

  • Transfer biodiesel to a clean flask
  • Add a small amount of warm distilled water
  • Shake gently and allow to separate
  • Repeat washing until the water is clear
6

Drying & Final Product

Time: 30-45 minutes

  • Allow biodiesel to dry before storage
  • Store in a clean, dry glass container
7

Quality Testing

Time: 20-30 minutes

  • Check clarity: biodiesel should be clear and bright yellow
  • Perform acid number test
  • Check flash point using flash point tester
  • Measure viscosity at 40°C
  • Record all observations and measurements

Observed Output

Biodiesel (top layer): 32 mL

Glycerol (bottom layer): 28 mL

Percentage Yield = (Mass of Biodiesel Obtained / Initial Oil Mass) × 100

Chemical Reaction Overview

Triglyceride (Castor Oil) + 3 CH₃OH → 3 RCOOCH₃ (Biodiesel) + C₃H₈O₃ (Glycerol)

Where:

  • Triglyceride = Castor oil
  • CH₃OH = Methanol
  • RCOOCH₃ = Fatty Acid Methyl Esters (Biodiesel)
  • C₃H₈O₃ = Glycerol (Byproduct)

Observed products: Biodiesel 32 mL and glycerol 28 mL.

Optimized Reaction Conditions

Parameter Value Unit Effect
Temperature 50-60 °C Enhances reaction rate
Ultrasonic Frequency 40-45 kHz Improves mixing
Methanol Ratio 6:1 mol/mol Optimal conversion
Catalyst Concentration 1.5-2 wt% Accelerates reaction
Reaction Time 20-30 min Complete conversion

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about biodiesel production and the experiment

Biodiesel is a renewable fuel made from vegetable oils, animal fats, or recycled cooking oil. It's produced through a process called transesterification, where oils are chemically converted into fatty acid methyl esters that can be used in diesel engines.

Ultrasonic waves create cavitation bubbles that enhance mixing and mass transfer, reducing reaction time from hours to minutes. This increases efficiency, improves yield, and reduces energy consumption compared to traditional mechanical stirring.

With proper experimental technique, you can expect a yield of 90-95%. The yield depends on oil quality, catalyst concentration, temperature, and reaction time. Some loss occurs during washing and drying steps.

Yes! Animal fats (tallow, lard), waste cooking oil, and algae oil can all be used. However, their free fatty acid content varies, which affects the catalyst requirement. High acid content oils may require acid esterification first.

Glycerin (glycerol) is a valuable byproduct that can be used in pharmaceuticals, cosmetics, and other applications. It can be purified further through distillation for commercial use.

Catalyst concentration should be 1.5-2% by weight of oil. Too little catalyst slows the reaction; too much can cause side reactions and affect product purity. The optimal range maximizes conversion while maintaining quality.

Washing removes unreacted methanol, catalyst residues, and soap formed during the reaction. These impurities can affect fuel quality, corrosion properties, and engine performance. Multiple washing cycles ensure purity.

The ideal temperature is 50-60°C. This range balances reaction speed with methanol evaporation prevention. Below 50°C, the reaction is slower; above 60°C, methanol loss increases, reducing yield.

Yes! Ethanol can be used as an alternative. It produces ethyl esters instead of methyl esters. However, ethanol requires higher temperatures and longer reaction times. The final product properties are slightly different but still viable as biodiesel.

Biodiesel reduces CO₂ emissions by up to 78% compared to fossil diesel, is biodegradable, non-toxic, and supports sustainable agriculture. It reduces dependence on fossil fuels and can help combat climate change.

Biodiesel should be stored in clean, dry containers in a cool place away from light. It can absorb water and oxidize if exposed to air and light. Proper storage maintains fuel quality for longer periods.
